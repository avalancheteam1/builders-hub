**By the end of this lesson you will be able to:**

- Locate where a circuit's constraints actually go
- Apply the optimisation levers in order of real impact
- Judge when batching or a custom L1 changes the economics

A private transfer on the teaching contract from the previous lesson costs somewhere between 300,000 and 400,000 gas. Verification is most of it — 16 public inputs put it near 288,000 — and inside that, 181,000 gas is a hard floor no amount of Solidity cleverness gets below, because it is the price of the pairing precompile itself.

<Callout type="info">
**Production eERC costs substantially more, and the gap is instructive.** Its transfer circuit carries 32 public signals rather than 16, so verification alone is about 387,000 gas by the Module 2 formula, and a full `transfer` measures **about 947,000 gas** on C-Chain mainnet in eERC's own benchmark suite. The extra goes on the auditor ciphertext, the encrypted-summary (PCT) storage, and the balance-hash bookkeeping — none of which the teaching contract has.

Every number in this lesson describes the teaching contract unless it says otherwise. The real figures are tabulated at the end, and the ratio is the thing to internalise: a feature that adds public inputs adds 6,000 gas each to *every transaction forever*, which is why the auditor requirement roughly doubles the cost of the entire operation.
</Callout>

So optimization here isn't about shaving off a few percent. It's about restructuring so that fewer verifications happen at all, which moves the number by an order of magnitude.

## Where the Gas Goes

Start by knowing what you're optimizing:

| Component | Gas | Reducible? |
|---|---|---|
| `ecPairing`, 4 pairs | 181,000 | No, this is the floor |
| `ecMul` for public inputs | ~6,000 each | Yes, fewer public inputs |
| Calldata | ~4,100 | Yes, point compression |
| Balance storage writes | 20,000 new / 5,000 update | Yes, events instead of storage |
| BabyJubJub point addition | several thousand each | Yes, precompile on a custom L1 |
| Solidity overhead | ~5,000 | Marginally |

The pairing dominates and cannot be reduced. Everything below is therefore either about amortizing it across more transactions, or about the parts that are not the pairing.

## Proof Aggregation

**Aggregation** replaces N proofs with one proof that attests to all of them.

An aggregator, either a relayer service or a contract, collects pending transactions with their individual proofs. It then generates a new proof whose circuit contains N copies of the verification algorithm, proving "I checked all N of these and they passed."

On-chain, only that single proof is verified.

| Approach | Gas for 100 transfers (teaching contract, 16 public inputs) |
|---|---|
| Individual verification | ~28,800,000 |
| One aggregated proof | ~300,000 |

Per transaction, that takes verification from 288,000 gas down to roughly 3,000.

### What it costs you

- **Latency.** Transactions wait for a batch to fill. Users see a delay between submitting and finality that they did not have before.
- **Aggregator position.** The aggregator sees every pending transaction. Private inputs remain hidden, but it can censor, reorder, or simply go offline.
- **Prover cost.** The aggregation circuit contains N verifiers, which is enormous. Generating that proof needs real hardware and takes minutes, which Module 5 quantifies.

Aggregation is the largest single win available, and it also comes with the most operational baggage.

## Batch Verification

Batch verification is a different technique, frequently confused with aggregation, and much simpler to deploy.

**Batch verification** does not create a new proof. It exploits a property of pairing checks: given N Groth16 proofs, a verifier can pick random scalars `r₁ … rₙ`, form a random linear combination of the proofs, and check a single combined pairing equation. It passes only if every individual check would have passed, except with negligible probability.

What this does **not** do is collapse the work to a constant. Each proof carries its own `B` point in G2, so the combined equation looks like:

```
Π e(rᵢ·Aᵢ, Bᵢ) = e(Σrᵢ·α, β) · e(Σrᵢ·Lᵢ, γ) · e(Σrᵢ·Cᵢ, δ)
```

That is one `ecPairing` call, but with **N+3 pairs**, not 4. The saving comes from paying the 45,000 gas base cost once and skipping the per-proof Solidity overhead, not from eliminating the pairings themselves.

The cost becomes:

- Roughly 2N `ecMul` on G1, randomizing each `Aᵢ` and `Cᵢ`: ~12,000N gas
- One `ecPairing` call with N+3 pairs: 45,000 + 34,000(N+3)

| Batch size | Individual | Batched | Saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1,440,000 | ~380,000 | 74% |
| 10 | 2,880,000 | ~610,000 | 79% |
| 50 | 14,400,000 | ~2,450,000 | 83% |
| 100 | 28,800,000 | ~4,750,000 | 84% |

The saving climbs quickly and then flattens in the low 80s, because at large N the cost is dominated by the 34,000 gas per pair, plus the randomizing `ecMul`s, no matter how you arrange it. Getting to a genuinely constant-size check requires the inner-pairing-product machinery in SnarkPack, which is a different construction with its own setup requirements.

<Callout type="warn">
The batched column above counts the pairing and the randomizing `ecMul`s only. It leaves out public-input processing, which does **not** batch away: the combined equation still needs each proof's public-input term, so you pay roughly 6,000 gas per public input per proof regardless. On a 16-input transfer that is another ~96,000 gas per proof, which dominates everything else in the table and cuts the real saving well below these figures.

Batch verification is therefore most attractive for circuits with *few* public inputs, and packing public inputs (below) compounds with it rather than competing. Benchmark your own circuit before planning around any of these numbers.
</Callout>

Even so, the appeal is that it requires no new circuit, no new ceremony, and no aggregator infrastructure. The verifier contract implements it directly.

<Callout type="warn">
**Where the random scalars come from is a security decision, not a detail.** If whoever assembles the batch can choose or foresee them, they can craft a set of individually invalid proofs whose linear combination satisfies the combined equation — and the batch verifies while containing forgeries.

**Do not use a block hash.** `blockhash(block.number - 1)` is already known when the submitter builds their transaction, so they can grind proof sets against it offline until one passes. The current block's hash isn't available to a contract at all, and on any chain where a proposer has influence over inclusion, it isn't neutral either.

What does work:

- **Derive the scalars from the batch contents**, as `keccak256` over every proof and public input in the batch, so changing any proof changes every scalar and there is nothing to grind against.
- **Commit and reveal**, where the batch is committed in one transaction and the randomness is fixed afterwards.
- **A verifiable randomness source** such as a VRF or a drand beacon, where the value provably could not have been known in advance.

The first option is usually the right one: it is cheap, needs no external dependency, and a submitter cannot manipulate the scalars without invalidating the batch they are trying to pass.
</Callout>

## Calldata Costs

C-Chain charges 16 gas per non-zero calldata byte and 4 per zero byte.

A Groth16 proof as eight 32-byte words is 256 bytes, roughly 4,100 gas. The public inputs add more, and for a private transfer, so do the ciphertexts: each ElGamal ciphertext is four field elements, so 128 bytes per ciphertext, and a transfer carries two.

Three things help:

**Point compression.** A G1 point is determined by its x coordinate plus one bit of sign. Transmitting compressed halves the calldata for proof elements, at the cost of decompression work on-chain, which involves a square root in the field. Whether it nets out depends on your calldata-to-computation ratio, so measure it instead of assuming.

**Packing public inputs.** If public signals are small values instead of full field elements, several fit in one 256-bit word. This also reduces the `ecMul` count in the verifier, which is the bigger saving.

**Events instead of storage.** Log data costs 8 gas per byte, against 20,000 for a fresh storage slot. If off-chain indexers can reconstruct what they need from events, emitting ciphertexts instead of storing them is a substantial win. The contract keeps only what it must read on-chain.

## Circuit Optimization

Reducing constraints does **not** reduce verification gas. Groth16 verification is constant-time and constant-cost regardless of circuit size.

What it does reduce is proving time, proving key size, memory footprint on the client, and the cost of any aggregation layer built on top. Those all matter, just not on the line item you might expect.

The levers, in rough order of impact:

- **Poseidon instead of SHA-256**, which the hash lesson covered at 240 constraints against 25,000.
- **Batched range checks.** One circuit validating several values at once beats independent checks per value.
- **Compiler optimization.** `circom --O2` removes some redundant constraints automatically. Check the before-and-after count instead of assuming it helped.
- **Lookup tables**, if you are on PLONK instead of Groth16. Range checks that cost hundreds of constraints become single lookups.

Finding out where your constraints actually go takes a little more work than it should. `circom --inspect` is a *correctness* check: it warns about signals that are assigned but never constrained, which is the bug class from the Circom lesson. It does not break the count down by template.

For the distribution, compile with `--r1cs` and read the total from `snarkjs r1cs info`, then compile variants with templates commented out and diff the totals. Tedious, but it gives you real numbers instead of guesses. In a typical eERC-style circuit the distribution comes out roughly:

- ElGamal operations, mostly BabyJubJub scalar multiplication: 40–60%
- Range checks: 15–25%
- Wiring and control logic: 10–20%
- Poseidon hashing: 5–10%

That distribution tells you where to spend your time. Optimizing the Poseidon calls in a circuit that's 50% scalar multiplication is effort spent on the wrong half.

## What Avalanche Gives You

**Low base fees.** The C-Chain minimum base fee is 1 wei, set by [ACP-176](/docs/acps/176-dynamic-evm-gas-limit-and-price-discovery-updates) in the April 2025 Fortuna upgrade. That floor is a lower bound rather than a typical price — the base fee moves with demand — but under normal load it sits well below 1 nAVAX, which puts a 250,000 gas transaction at a small fraction of a cent. This doesn't change any of the ratios above, but it changes which applications are viable at all.

**Fast finality.** Sub-second finality shortens the window for the front-running and reordering issues from the previous lesson, and it means a user is not staring at a pending spinner after already having waited for proof generation.

**Custom L1 precompiles.** The largest structural lever available. An L1 can add native implementations of the operations that are expensive in Solidity:

| Operation | In Solidity | As a precompile |
|---|---|---|
| Poseidon hash | 20,000–50,000 gas | low thousands |
| BabyJubJub scalar multiplication | very expensive | low thousands |
| BabyJubJub point addition | several thousand | hundreds |
| STARK verification | millions | plausibly hundreds of thousands |

<Callout type="info">
Precompile gas costs are set by whoever configures the L1, benchmarked against the native implementation's actual cost. The figures above are the order of magnitude you should expect, not fixed constants, and the real numbers depend on the implementation and the pricing policy the chain adopts.
</Callout>

Note which operations appear on that list. Point addition is exactly what the previous lesson's homomorphic balance update spends its gas on, and Poseidon hashing is what an on-chain Merkle root update spends its gas on. A ZK-heavy application on C-Chain is paying Solidity prices for both.

## Benchmarks

**Estimates for the teaching contract**, derived from the precompile costs above:

| Operation | Gas on C-Chain |
|---|---|
| Standard ERC-20 transfer | 65,000 |
| Groth16 verification, 1 public input | ~196,000 |
| Groth16 verification, 16 public inputs | ~288,000 |
| Private transfer, verify plus homomorphic update | 300,000–400,000 |
| Batch verification, 10 proofs | ~610,000 |
| Batch verification, 50 proofs | ~2,450,000 |
| Merkle root update, depth 20, Poseidon in Solidity | ~500,000 |

**Measured figures for production eERC**, from its own benchmark suite on Avalanche mainnet (Solidity 0.8.27, optimizer on, 200 runs):

| Method | Public signals | Avg gas |
|---|---|---|
| `Registrar.register` | 5 | 322,143 |
| `deposit` | — (no proof) | 564,892 |
| `privateMint` | 24 | 722,016 |
| `withdraw` | 16 | 796,263 |
| `transfer` | 32 | **947,313** |
| `privateBurn` | 19 | 1,028,678 |

Read those two tables against each other, because the comparison is the lesson. A real private transfer is about **fourteen times** a plain ERC-20 transfer, and roughly two and a half times the teaching contract — most of that difference bought by the auditor requirement and the encrypted-summary storage, not by anything inefficient. Public signal count tracks cost closely, which is the single most actionable thing on this page.

Then compare the Merkle row against the precompile costs above. The same operation at precompile speed is an order of magnitude cheaper, and it runs on every insertion. That line is most of the argument for building a ZK application on a dedicated L1 instead of C-Chain, which is where Module 5 picks up.
